# Your Software Knows More Than You Do

I’ve never really liked most CRM or FSM software.

Not because software is bad, and not because I think these companies are evil. Most of them are
probably filled with smart people trying to build useful products while also keeping a business
profitable. I understand why SaaS exists. I understand recurring revenue. I understand why
companies centralize services in the cloud.

But lately I’ve started wondering if anyone else has noticed how strange this has become.

Modern software platforms don’t just help run our businesses anymore. They observe them. And in
many cases, they probably understand our industries better than we do.

## The View From Above

An HVAC company sees its own jobs, callbacks, pricing, customers, and technicians. A software
platform sees thousands of companies operating at once. It sees millions of invoices, regional
pricing trends, equipment failure patterns, replacement rates, technician notes, customer
communication habits, seasonal demand shifts, and sales conversion behavior.

That’s a completely different level of visibility.

Ten years ago, most data collection felt like marketing. Now it feels more like infrastructure.
AI changed the weight and value of operational data. Information that once sat in databases
mostly unused can now be analyzed, summarized, categorized, correlated, and turned into
something strategically valuable.

That’s the part I can’t stop thinking about.

## The Software May Not Be The Product Anymore

We all assume the software company’s primary product is the software itself. But what if the
most valuable thing they actually have is the aggregated understanding created by all of us
using it?

Not my company specifically. Not your company specifically. The patterns that emerge when
thousands of companies all operate inside the same platform.

And this isn’t even a new idea anymore. We’ve already watched this happen across the internet.

Social media companies started as communication platforms, then realized the behavioral data
was incredibly valuable. Google became powerful not just because of search, but because it
understood what billions of people were looking for. Amazon doesn’t just sell products — it has
one of the most detailed views of purchasing behavior ever assembled.

In all of those cases, the platform became more valuable because of the visibility created by
the people using it.

That aggregated visibility is incredibly powerful. And honestly, it would be strange if
companies _didn’t_ see value in it. If you had access to operational patterns across an entire
industry, why wouldn’t you analyze them? Why wouldn’t you use them to improve products, develop
AI tools, guide automation, or shape future business decisions?

The strange part is that despite having unprecedented visibility into entire industries, most
CRM/FSM software still feels oddly disconnected from the actual work.

Most of these platforms try to serve HVAC, plumbing, electrical, cleaning, landscaping,
roofing, pest control, and twenty other industries all at once. The result is usually software
that becomes broad instead of deep. The workflows feel generic, the features feel shallow, and
the AI tools often feel bolted on rather than genuinely useful.

That disconnect feels increasingly strange to me. These companies are sitting on enormous
amounts of operational knowledge, yet the software often still feels like it was designed by
people optimizing spreadsheets instead of supporting actual field work.

## We Already Watched This Happen To Equipment

Tradespeople have already seen versions of this elsewhere.

Tools became subscriptions. Diagnostics moved behind dealer portals. Repair information
disappeared into proprietary apps. Features started requiring cloud accounts and manufacturer
approval.

Now we’re doing the same thing to the software that runs our businesses. Except this time the
product isn’t just access. It’s visibility.

Most software companies will tell you that you own your data, and technically that’s probably
true. You can usually export it. You can usually leave. I’m not really arguing about lock-in.

What I keep coming back to is something different: we increasingly have no meaningful
visibility into what our data becomes while it’s there.

Not necessarily because anyone is doing something malicious. In fact, I think most companies
are probably acting within exactly the terms we agreed to. The problem is that almost nobody
fully understands the implications of those agreements anymore because the value of data itself
changed underneath us.

Operational knowledge is no longer just stored information. It’s training material. It’s
pattern recognition. It’s market intelligence. It’s automation fuel.

And I don’t think most businesses have fully processed that shift yet.

## I Care More About Visibility Than Licensing

When I talk about open source or source-available software, I’m not making a licensing
argument. I’m not arguing every company should give away their software for free, and I’m not
pretending every contractor suddenly wants to self-host infrastructure.

I care much more about inspectability than ideology.

I think software that stores critical operational or customer data should probably be
observable in some meaningful way. Not because every business owner is going to audit source
code, but because blind trust feels like an increasingly outdated security model.

Especially now that software platforms are becoming more intelligent, more centralized, and
more capable of understanding the industries they serve.

## The Speed Problem

There’s another shift happening that I think is connected to all of this.

Software companies are shipping faster than ever now, largely because AI coding tools
dramatically lowered the cost and effort required to generate code. Features that might have
taken weeks to prototype a few years ago can now be assembled in days or even hours.

On paper, that sounds great.

In practice, a lot of modern software increasingly feels like it’s in a permanent beta state.

Updates roll out constantly. Interfaces change for no obvious reason. Features appear
half-finished. Regressions show up in workflows that were working perfectly fine a month
earlier. Entire sections of software suddenly behave differently because some AI-assisted
refactor touched code the original developers barely understand themselves anymore.

And to be clear, I’m not anti-AI. I use AI tools constantly, they can be incredibly useful.

But I think we’re entering a phase where software complexity is growing faster than human
understanding of the systems being built. AI dramatically increases the amount of code
companies can produce, but it doesn’t automatically increase the amount of care, context,
testing, or long-term ownership behind that code.

That disconnect matters when software becomes infrastructure for real businesses.

Years ago, buggy software was frustrating. Today, a broken update can affect dispatching,
invoicing, payroll, customer communication, scheduling, inventory, or an entire operational
workflow.

This is another reason I’ve become more interested in source-available systems and
inspectability.

Not because I think every business owner wants to become a software engineer, but because the
ability to observe, audit, modify, or even propose fixes to software feels increasingly
important in a world where the pace of software generation is accelerating faster than the pace
of software understanding.

Especially now that many of us also have access to AI tools ourselves.

For the first time, smaller companies and individuals can realistically inspect systems, trace
behavior, suggest fixes, write patches, automate workflows, or adapt software to their needs
without needing a massive engineering team behind them.

That changes the equation.

The same AI systems making software harder to trust are also lowering the barrier to
understanding and improving it ourselves.

## Maybe We Should Think About Software Differently

Maybe none of this is malicious.

Maybe this is simply what happens when software becomes infrastructure, data becomes
strategically valuable, and AI dramatically increases the ability to analyze, generate, and
automate around that data.

But I still think we crossed an important line somewhere.

Software used to feel like a tool you operated. Increasingly, it feels like a system that
observes the people using it.

And that shift matters more than I think most businesses realize.

The same platforms we depend on to schedule jobs, communicate with customers, process invoices,
dispatch technicians, and run operations are also accumulating an unprecedented understanding
of the industries they serve. At the same time, software itself is becoming harder to
understand, changing faster, and increasingly generated by systems that can produce code far
quicker than humans can realistically reason about it.

That combination creates an uncomfortable dynamic.

The people generating the operational knowledge often have the least visibility into how the
systems around them actually work.

I don’t think the answer is abandoning technology, rejecting AI, or pretending we can go
backwards. I use modern software and AI tools constantly. They’re incredibly useful.

But I do think we need to start treating software differently.

Not just as convenience. Not just as subscriptions. Not just as apps.

But as critical infrastructure that increasingly shapes industries, concentrates visibility,
and influences the people depending on it.

And if that’s true, then inspectability, transparency, and source availability stop feeling
like fringe technical ideals.

They start feeling practical.
