# The Quiet Things Grief Does To You

Tomorrow marks one year since Alicia passed away.

Honestly, that still doesn’t even sound real when I say it out loud.

People tend to think grief is this loud thing. Crying all the time. Breaking down constantly.
Some dramatic collapse. Sometimes it is. But more often than not, grief is quiet. It lives in
ordinary moments, hides in routines, in smells, and silence. In the realization that someone
who once filled every corner of your life is suddenly nowhere physically to be found, yet
somehow still everywhere.

I smell the lavender floor cleaner Alicia used and immediately think of her.

I still avoid the room where her ashes are sometimes because I’m just not ready. I still
haven’t spread them. I thought by now I would have figured out the “right” thing to do, but
grief doesn’t really work on timelines no matter how badly we want it to.

The strange thing is that it’s not always the big moments that hurt the most, it’s weekends,
sitting in the house knowing she should be in the other room, hearing the dogs moving around
and remembering how much of their care and discipline she handled. Realizing how many invisible
things she quietly carried every single day without asking for recognition.

She made sure I ate. She interrupted my tendency to isolate myself from the world. She took
care of the house, the dogs, the details, and me. Not in some dramatic movie kind of way. The
real way, the everyday way. The kind of love that becomes so woven into your life that you stop
noticing how much weight the other person is carrying until suddenly you’re forced to carry it
alone.

That’s one of the hardest parts about losing a partner. Not just missing them emotionally, but
realizing how much of your life was built around their presence.

And I want to be very clear, I absolutely appreciated Alicia while she was here. I knew how
lucky I was! I knew who she was, and the kind of strength she had. I knew what she meant to me,
I just don’t always know if I showed her that as well as I should have! I think that’s probably
a very human regret. Not failing to love someone, but wishing you had expressed it more clearly
while you still had the chance.

Alicia was proud of our home, proud of our life together, proud of the things we built. Even
though this house existed long before us, she made it ours. Now every room feels tied to her in
some way except maybe my chair ;)

People often ask grieving people, “How are you doing?” I understand why they ask it. Most
people genuinely care and just don’t know what else to say. But it’s a difficult question
because the honest answer is usually not socially acceptable.

I’m not okay!

At the same time, life keeps moving, the business still runs, the dogs still need cared for.
The bills still show up, people still expect you to function. So eventually you learn how to
become operational again long before you actually feel whole again.

I’ve also realized grief has a strange contradiction to it. I want to be left alone, except for
when I don’t, and somehow I expect everyone else to magically know the difference. I know that
makes no sense, but grief rarely does. Isolation has always been an easy place for me to drift
toward, and grief gives you a thousand excuses to stay there if you let it.

That’s one thing I hope Alicia would actually be proud of me for. I didn’t disappear into
alcohol the way I might have years ago. I didn’t completely self-destruct. That doesn’t mean
this has been graceful or healthy all the time, it hasn’t, but I’m still here, still trying,
still moving forward even on the days I don’t particularly want to.

I think one of the fears nobody talks about enough is not just losing someone, but slowly
feeling the intensity of the world’s attention move on afterward. Right after someone dies
there are flowers, calls, support, services, gatherings, rituals. Then eventually the dust
settles and everyone returns to their lives while you’re still waking up every day trying to
understand how yours changed so completely.

But I’m also beginning to understand that honoring someone isn’t about staying frozen in the
worst moment of your life forever. It isn’t about performing grief loudly enough to prove your
love was real. The love was already real! The life was real! Alicia was real!

I think the best way I can honor her now is simply to continue trying to become someone she
would still feel proud of choosing.

Not perfect. Not polished. Not fearless.

Just honest.

And if there’s one thing I know for certain, Alicia respected honesty.

I miss you every single day.

I love you still.
